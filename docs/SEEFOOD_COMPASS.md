# SeeFood Compass — draft for alignment

**Purpose of this document:** a plain-language working agreement. It is not a
roadmap approval or a promise that every idea below will be built. Please edit
the words directly until they describe what we actually want.

## 1. Current purpose

SeeFood's first and primary use case is for a person who is already at a
restaurant. They open SeeFood, the app recognizes where they are, and it shows
useful photos of that restaurant's food so they can decide what to order.

The second use case is discovery. A person can use SeeFood to decide where to
eat and explore dishes at other restaurants. Discovery matters, but it follows
the at-the-restaurant ordering experience rather than competing with it for
first priority.

In both cases, useful food photos should be linked to the correct restaurant
and dish, and the person should be able to understand whether a photo came from
the restaurant or from a customer.

The product is valuable only when its information is trustworthy enough to
help with a real dining decision. A large count of restaurants, menu items, or
photos is not success if the photos are duplicates, wrongly matched, stale, or
not useful food.

## 2. Proposed short-term goals

For now, the main job is to make Temecula a credible proof market and keep the
live experience honest.

1. Make the primary diner experience dependable: when someone is already at a
   restaurant, SeeFood recognizes the correct place and immediately shows its
   visual food choices to help them decide what to order.
2. Improve useful coverage, not vanity totals: accurate restaurant identity,
   current menus, distinct food photos, and strong dish-photo matches.
3. Protect trust as data grows: preserve source information, remove or prevent
   harmful duplicates, avoid weak claims, and make changes reversible.
4. Measure readiness plainly: distinguish verified restaurants, live
   restaurants, strong restaurants, and neighborhood availability rather than
   collapsing them into one impressive-sounding number.
5. Support the second use case with a clear map and restaurant-discovery
   experience. Thin but real restaurants may be visible; they should not be
   presented as richly covered.

## 3. Proposed long-term goals

If the Temecula experience proves useful, SeeFood can grow into a reliable
visual ordering and food-discovery service that works across markets.

1. Build the most useful visual ordering experience for people already at a
   restaurant: accurate location recognition followed by compelling, useful
   dish choices.
2. Expand one market at a time, only when the quality and operating process
   hold up—not merely because a large restaurant list can be imported.
3. Make discovery a strong second experience for people choosing where to eat
   or exploring dishes beyond the restaurant they are currently visiting.
4. Accumulate a durable, evidence-based restaurant, menu, and photo record
   that gets better with careful updates instead of starting over each visit.
5. Eventually enable two clearly labeled additions to the record: customer
   contributions and verified restaurant-provided photos. These are potential
   growth paths, not current commitments.
6. Create a business that can maintain accurate coverage, respect provenance
   and permissions, and remain useful as outside sources change.

## 4. Non-goals and boundaries (for the current phase)

- We are not trying to list every business in America as quickly as possible.
- We do not count raw photos, scraped records, or AI guesses as proof of useful
  dish coverage.
- We do not show prices in the early product, even when price data is captured.
- We are not building native mobile apps; SeeFood remains a web app for now.
- We are not assuming a full rebuild is the strategy. Improve the current
  product unless a separate decision approves a rebuild.
- We are not treating the research lab as a second product team or giving it
  authority to change production. Its current acquisition program is complete;
  new outside-data work needs a deliberate decision.
- We should not scale aggressive scraping, paid data sources, or new external
  commitments without clear permission, cost, rights, and quality gates.
- We do not hide uncertain or weak data behind a polished interface.

## 5. Assumptions, tensions, and questions to settle

The following are deliberately unresolved. They need a product-owner answer,
not an implicit technical choice.

1. **What proves the primary experience works?** When a diner is already at a
   restaurant, how reliably must SeeFood recognize the correct place, and how
   useful must its visual dish coverage be, before Temecula is a success?
2. **What should discovery do after the primary experience is dependable?**
   Which map and browsing behaviors best help a person choose where to eat
   without pulling focus from the at-the-restaurant ordering experience?
3. **How broad should the map be before its content is deep?** The current
   approach favors showing real but thin restaurants honestly. Is that the
   desired user promise, or should discovery be more selective until coverage
   is stronger?
4. **How should expansion be earned?** Should Temecula exit criteria be based
   on user validation, data-quality thresholds, operating cost, revenue signs,
   or a defined combination?
5. **Are customer and merchant contributions a future strategic bet or merely
   an option to keep open?** Their value depends on trust, moderation,
   permission, and active participation—not just shipping an upload button.
6. **What is the business model hypothesis?** A claimed restaurant page is a
   possible future path, but it is not yet proof that restaurants will pay or
   that it should drive product priorities.
7. **What is the acceptable boundary for outside data?** The current evidence
   supports cautious, provenance-preserving acquisition. More ambitious access
   to delivery-platform data would require a separately authorized business
   and rights decision.
